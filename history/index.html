A history of amateur radio

Amateur radio is older than broadcasting, older than commercial aviation, almost as old as powered flight. For more than a century, amateurs — licensed hobbyists experimenting at their own expense — have pushed radio forward, opened new frequencies, and, again and again, kept communities connected when everything else failed. This is the story of how it grew.

Before the amateurs: the wave is proven

In 1888 the German physicist Heinrich Hertz proved that James Clerk Maxwell's predicted electromagnetic waves were real, generating and detecting them in his laboratory. Within a decade Guglielmo Marconi, building on the work of Hertz, Oliver Lodge, Alexander Popov, Nikola Tesla and others, turned the phenomenon into a practical system, and in 1901 reported spanning the Atlantic with wireless signals. What had been a laboratory curiosity was suddenly a way to communicate without wires.

The first amateurs (1900s–1912)

As soon as the equipment could be built at home, curious experimenters built it. Magazines published construction articles; by 1904 two Boston schoolboys had a working transmitter with a range of several miles, and clubs began to form — the Columbia University Wireless Telegraph Club, recorded in 1908, is among the earliest. These were true amateurs in the original sense: unpaid, self-taught, and often astonishingly capable.

Regulation arrives: the Radio Act of 1912

The unregulated airwaves grew crowded and, after the RMS Titanic disaster in 1912 exposed the chaos of competing wireless signals, the United States passed the Radio Act of 1912. Amateurs were licensed for the first time and pushed to "200 meters and down" (1500 kHz and above) — wavelengths then thought useless for long distance. The number of hobbyists is estimated to have dropped sharply. It was meant as a banishment. It became a gift.

The ARRL and the relay idea (1914)

In 1914, inventor Hiram Percy Maxim and Clarence Tuska founded the American Radio Relay League (ARRL) in Hartford, Connecticut. The idea was simple and powerful: individual stations had short range, but a message could be relayed from station to station across a continent. Organized relay networks turned scattered hobbyists into a communication system — and gave the League its enduring name.

World War I and the shortwave breakthrough (1917–1924)

When the United States entered the First World War in 1917, amateurs were ordered off the air and their equipment dismantled; operation resumed only in 1919. Freed again, amateurs turned to the "useless" short waves — and discovered they were anything but. In late 1921 the ARRL sent Paul Godley to Scotland to listen for American stations; he copied dozens, proving transatlantic amateur work was possible. By November 1923 a two-way contact was completed between Fred Schnell (1MO) in the U.S. and Léon Deloy (8AB) in France. The realization that short waves could circle the globe with modest power reshaped all of radio.

Bands, callsigns and new modes (1925–1939)

International coordination followed the technology. In 1925 amateurs founded the International Amateur Radio Union (IARU) in Paris to represent their interests worldwide. The 1927 International Radiotelegraph Conference in Washington established standard amateur bands — 80, 40, 20 and 10 meters — and the system of national callsign prefixes still used today. Through the 1930s amateurs pioneered single-sideband voice and pushed ever higher in frequency.

World War II and the postwar boom (1939–1960)

The Second World War again silenced amateur transmissions in belligerent nations, while thousands of operators served as radio technicians and instructors — skills the hobby had given them. After 1945, war-surplus equipment and a generation trained in electronics fueled an enormous boom. Single sideband matured into the dominant HF voice mode through the 1950s.

Reaching space: OSCAR and beyond (1961–)

On 12 December 1961, just four years after Sputnik, amateurs launched OSCAR I — the first amateur satellite and one of the first non-government satellites of any kind. A long line of OSCARs followed, and today amateurs communicate through orbiting transponders and even from the International Space Station. In 1979 the WARC conference granted the 30, 17 and 12-meter bands, still known as the "WARC bands".

The digital turn (1980s–today)

Packet radio in the 1980s, PSK31 and sound-card modes around 2000, and then Joe Taylor's WSJT family — culminating in FT8 (2017) — transformed operating. Weak-signal digital modes decode signals buried far below the noise, letting modest stations work the world. In 2007 the U.S. dropped the Morse-code requirement for licensing, reflecting a hobby that had become as much about software and experimentation as about the key.

Amateur radio in Brazil

Brazil's amateurs organized early too: the Liga de Amadores Brasileiros de Rádio Emissão (LABRE) was founded in 1934 and remains the national society and IARU member. Brazilian callsigns carry the prefixes PP–PY (and PT, PU, PV, PW), with the PU block used for the entry-level class under which many operators, including PU4FOS, begin. Today the service is regulated by Anatel.

Why it still matters

Amateur radio is not nostalgia. When hurricanes, earthquakes and floods take down cellular networks and the internet, amateurs provide independent, infrastructure-free communication — as they did during Hurricane Katrina (2005), the 2010 Haiti earthquake, and countless local emergencies, and as organized services such as ARES and RAYNET are trained to do. A mode of communication that needs no towers, no data centres and no permission to exist is a form of resilience: a redundant channel that stays up precisely when the complex systems we depend on go down. That, more than any single contact, is why more than three million people worldwide still hold a licence.
